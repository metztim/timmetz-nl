03 Rhythm

Work Rhythm

Timed sessions, breaks, and a printed sheet for the day.

How I cut up the day decides how well I focus. I work in timed sessions, take the break when each session ends, and plan the day on one printed page.

Timed sessions

Before I start, I decide what the one task is and how long the session runs: 25 minutes, 50, or at most 90. Then I start a timer and do only that task until the timer ends.

Sixty minutes on one task and sixty minutes split over five tasks are different hours. The first is the one that produces a draft.

Any timer works. I use Lifeline, a Mac app I built, which shows the session as a progress bar at the top of the screen. The Pomodoro Technique and other work rhythms compares the common session lengths.

Breaks on a ratio

For every five minutes of work I take one minute of rest, so a 50-minute session earns a 10-minute break. The break happens away from the screen: a walk, a book, or a chat.

Concentration runs in cycles of about 90 minutes. Past that point the quality of my thinking drops and the mistakes start, which is why no session runs longer.

Taking breaks can feel less productive because the hours go down, yet I get more done with fewer mistakes. The Magic Power of Breaks has the research.

A stop for AI sessions

Work with AI has its own trap. I tell myself a fix or a research question will take 10 minutes, and two hours later I'm still in it, because the model keeps making just enough progress that I can't walk away. The 10-minute version and the two-hour version look the same at minute zero.

A timer, a break, and a handoff note get me out.

  1. 1A hard timerSet before I start: 10, 25, or 50 minutes, picked on purpose.
  2. 2A forced breakWhen the timer ends I step away, with no exceptions.
  3. 3A handoff noteWhat I was doing, where I got to, and what is next, saved in a system I trust.

After five minutes away from the screen, one of three things is usually obvious: I'm making progress and want to keep going, I'm making progress on something that should not get today's time, or I've been chasing a ghost. Just One More Prompt tells the full story.

Waiting on AI is the second trap. An answer that takes minutes is too long to sit and watch and too short to do anything else well, so I end up switching tasks every few minutes. I stay in the draft and read Claude's answer at the next natural stop. AI's Maddening Multitasking Trap explains why.

Daily planning sheet

Next to my keyboard sits a printed sheet that tells me what I committed to for today. I keep it beside me all day and tick off items by hand.

The front of the sheet holds what I plan and what I track.

  • My tasks, grouped by priority, with empty lines for tasks that come up during the day.
  • One or two things I count every day, such as hours of sleep and cups of coffee.
  • One sentence about the day.

The back has blank lines for ideas, reflections, and tasks for tomorrow. The sheet stays at one page. A plan that needs more than one page is unrealistic.

In the evening I take a photo of the sheet. An AI agent in Claude Code reads my scribbles and prepares a fresh page for the next day, so nothing I wrote down gets lost. The paper half works without the agent: a one-page list for today, printed in the morning and kept beside the keyboard.

The sheet lets me step back from everything moving on the screen and ask one question: what did I commit to for today?
