03 Rhythm

Work Rhythm

Timed sessions, breaks, and a printed sheet for the day.

I work in timed sessions, take a break after each one, and plan the day on one printed page.

Timed sessions

Before I start, I decide what the one task is and how long the session runs: 25 minutes, 50, or at most 90. Then I start a timer and do only that task until it ends.

Any timer works. I use Lifeline, a Mac app I built, which shows the session as a progress bar at the top of the screen. The Pomodoro Technique and other work rhythms compares the common session lengths.

Breaks on a ratio

For every five minutes of work I take one minute of rest, so a 50-minute session earns a 10-minute break away from the screen: a walk, a book, or a chat.

Concentration runs in cycles of about 90 minutes. Past that point my thinking gets worse and the mistakes start. With breaks the hours go down, yet I get more done. The Magic Power of Breaks has the research.

A stop for AI sessions

Work with AI has its own trap. I tell myself a fix will take 10 minutes, and two hours later I'm still in it, because the model keeps making just enough progress that I can't walk away. A timer, a break, and a handoff note get me out.

  1. 1A hard timerSet before I start: 10, 25, or 50 minutes.
  2. 2A forced breakWhen the timer ends I step away, with no exceptions.
  3. 3A handoff noteWhat I was doing, where I got to, and what is next.

After five minutes away from the screen, I usually know whether to keep going, stop, or admit I've been chasing a ghost. Just One More Prompt tells the full story.

Waiting on AI is the second trap. An answer that takes minutes is too long to watch and too short to do anything else well. I stay in the draft and read Claude's answer at the next natural stop. AI's Maddening Multitasking Trap explains why.

Daily planning sheet

Next to my keyboard sits a printed sheet with what I committed to for today. I tick off items by hand.

  • My tasks, grouped by priority, with empty lines for tasks that come up during the day.
  • One or two things I count every day, such as hours of sleep and cups of coffee.
  • One sentence about the day.

The back has blank lines for ideas and tasks for tomorrow, and the sheet stays at one page. In the evening I take a photo of the sheet, and an AI agent in Claude Code reads my scribbles and prepares the next day's page.
