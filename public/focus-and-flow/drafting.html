01 Drafting

Drafting With AI

Three ways I use Claude around a draft I write myself.

The Animalz Policy for AI in Writing has one hard line: don't use AI to generate text from scratch. Research, feedback, and polish are open.

The three practices on this page fit the policy because the sentences stay mine. Claude looks things up, suggests a word, or types up edits I already made by hand.

Research while you write

I write in Claude Docs, on a page as blank as a new Google Doc. Claude writes nothing there. Its work starts when I get stuck on something small.

This happens to me a lot: I'm halfway through a paragraph and I'm sure I've read research about the point, or that some writer has a quote about it. I can write down the gist, but I don't know if I have it right. I used to leave myself a comment and keep going, and by the end of the draft I had a pile of things to check.

In Claude Docs I leave that comment for Claude ("can you find the real quote?", "which research backs this up?") and keep writing. Claude does the lookup while I finish the section.

The same works for a single word. When I can't come up with the best way to say something, I highlight the phrase and ask "is there a better way of saying this?". Then I pick an option or write my own.

  1. Open a Claude Doc and write your draft there.
  2. When a quote, a study, or a number is missing, write what you remember and leave a comment asking Claude to find it.
  3. Keep writing.
  4. When Claude answers, open the source it gives you and check the wording there.

Policy: rule 3 is "Fact-check everything". Claude's answer is a lead. The quote or the number goes into your draft after you have read it at the source.

Edit on paper

When I have a draft or an outline, I print it and take it somewhere away from my computer: a coffee shop, or the kitchen table. Then I polish and edit with a pen and markers.

At the computer I can only edit well in the morning. On paper I can still do it in the evening, as long as I find a quiet spot and can concentrate for a while. A printed page has no distractions, I can sit wherever is convenient, and I can mark everything up the way I want.

Afterward I take pictures of the pages and give them to Claude, which applies my edits to the draft. Claude Opus is good at reading my handwriting and at seeing what I meant by an arrow or a crossed-out line. I think Sonnet could do it too.

  1. Print the draft with wide margins and double line spacing.
  2. Edit by hand, away from the screen.
  3. Photograph every page and upload the photos to Claude with the draft.
  4. Ask Claude to apply your edits as written and to change nothing else.
  5. Read the result against your pages.

Draft by hand

The more extreme version is to write the entire draft by hand. I have mostly done this for personal essays, which I can write from the top of my head, so I don't know how well it works for a customer article. With a good 30% outline next to the notebook, I think it could.

Writing by hand feels slow. When I measure it, I write six to eight notebook pages in an hour, which is a lot more than it feels like in the moment.

Claude then transcribes the photos of my pages, and the first draft in the doc is the one I wrote.

Other allowed uses

The policy lists eight more ways to use AI within the guideline, from an industry language check to condensing research. The full list is in the policy.

The policy also asks you to edit out phrasing that reads as AI, even when a human wrote it. The list of AI writing patterns shows what to look for.
